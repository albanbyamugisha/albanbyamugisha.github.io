Every semester, countless university students sit for take-home tests, coursework, and projects. Most work hard, read widely, revise their work, and submit the best version of themselves.

Increasingly, though, doing that well can work against them.

Picture two students in the same coursework class.

Same programme. Same deadline. Same lecturer.

One spends the week in the library, reads beyond the recommended textbooks, drafts, edits, and submits a sharp, well-argued paper.

The other does the bare minimum, submits something average, and moves on.

Neither used AI.

But when the results come back, it is the hardworking student who gets summoned and asked whether AI wrote the paper, while the average submission attracts no suspicion.

Students in many places report experiences like this. It may not be universal, but even a few cases are enough to be a strange new penalty for excellence.

Tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot have made it possible to generate acceptable academic writing in seconds. But the existence of those tools should not turn every strong piece of student work into evidence of misconduct.

A good paper is not, by itself, proof of AI use.

And there is a reason we should be careful.

Turnitin itself acknowledges that its AI-writing model can misidentify human-written work, and says its AI report should not be used as the sole basis for taking action against a student. Other higher-education guidance makes the same point: AI detectors can produce false positives, and an AI score alone is not sufficient evidence for an academic-misconduct allegation.

The problem may be even sharper for some students. A 2023 Stanford study found that AI detectors frequently misclassified essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated. Students who write carefully, in a second or third language, can be the ones most at risk of a false flag.

That matters because the consequences of getting it wrong are serious.

A student accused of using AI is not simply being told that their writing needs improvement. They may have to defend their work, their credibility, and their academic integrity.

So universities everywhere should be asking themselves an important question:

Are we protecting academic integrity, or are we sometimes punishing academic excellence?

None of this means AI misuse is not happening on campuses. It clearly is, and lecturers are right to take academic integrity seriously.

But there is a difference between investigating possible AI misuse and treating quality itself as evidence of AI use.

Bright, disciplined students existed long before generative AI.

The student who reads three extra textbooks, rewrites their introduction twice, checks their references, and learns how to express an argument clearly is not doing anything suspicious.

They are doing what strong students have always done.

And we should not make students afraid that producing their best work will automatically put them under suspicion.

So what should universities do?

First, use AI-detection scores as a starting point, not a verdict.

A flag should lead to questions and further evidence, not an automatic conclusion.

Second, assess the process, not only the final PDF.

Drafts, revision history, research notes, and short oral follow-ups can give lecturers a much clearer picture of whether a student actually understands and produced their work.

Third, redesign assessment where necessary.

If AI has changed the nature of take-home assignments, perhaps the answer is not simply better detection tools. Perhaps it is better assessment design: assignments that require students to explain, apply, defend, and reflect on what they have learned.

And what can students do?

While the system catches up, students can protect themselves with a few simple habits:

Keep your drafts. Save early versions instead of overwriting them.

Use version history. Tools like Google Docs and Word automatically record how your work developed over time.

Save your research notes. Highlighted readings, annotated sources, and rough outlines all show your thinking.

Be ready to explain your work. If you can walk someone through your argument in your own words, that is powerful evidence that it is yours.

None of this should be necessary to prove honesty. But for now, it is a sensible safeguard.

The goal should not be to make students prove their innocence because they wrote too well.

The goal should be to create an academic environment where honest work is recognized and dishonest work is fairly investigated.

Bright and genuine students are not a myth.

They are in lecture halls right now, doing the work.

The least our institutions can do is give them a fair chance to prove it.
